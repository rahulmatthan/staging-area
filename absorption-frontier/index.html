The Absorption Frontier

Eleven posts for X and LinkedIn explaining the ORF essay by Rahul Matthan. Shown as they would appear in the timeline. Wording is a working draft; the animations loop.

RM
Rahul Matthan1 of 11

Generative AI earned $110 bn last year, growing three times faster than mobile or the internet did. Yet only 1 in 20 enterprise pilots has produced any value. That gap is the whole story. AI supremacy will not go to whoever builds the best model. It will go to whoever can absorb it. A thread on why.

RM
Rahul Matthan2 of 11

Intelligence has only ever lived in human heads. It takes twenty years to make a doctor, once that happens he can only be in one place at a time, and his expertise dies with him. AI makes intelligence divisible, copyable and cheap. Every technology that did that to a scarce factor rebuilt civilisation.

RM
Rahul Matthan3 of 11

But invention is not impact. Firms that cut headcount found AI costs more than the people it replaced, and AI has moved from too cheap to meter to too expensive to afford. The lag in diffusion is not unexpected.

RM
Rahul Matthan4 of 11

Why the lag? Because the gains never come from the new thing being cheaper. Power was 3% of a factory's costs, so swapping the steam engine for an electric motor saved little and transformed nothing. The real work is not inventing a technology. It is rebuilding the world to absorb it.

RM
Rahul Matthan5 of 11

I call this line the absorption frontier: the point at which an economy has reorganised itself enough to unlock a technology's gains. Behind it, you have the technology and struggle to benefit from it. Across it, the gains exceed anything you could have foreseen. For a firm, a sector or a country, crossing it should be our most important objective.

RM
Rahul Matthan6 of 11

So where does the AI frontier lie? AI works on data, and the data has to be ready for it. Three conditions: legible, actionable, verifiable. In plain words, AI must be able to read it, act on it, and know when it got it right.

RM
Rahul Matthan7 of 11

Why did AI transform coding so quickly? Because code is inherently verifiable: it either compiles or it doesn't, and the software industry has spent decades building the architecture of verification. For any other system to work in the same way it will need its own architecture of verification.

RM
Rahul Matthan8 of 11

Software crossed the frontier by accident. Version control made every line legible, open interfaces made every system reachable, and the testing infrastructure made outputs checkable, well before AI arrived. Today nearly a third of new US code now relies on AI. What software did by accident, every other sector must do on purpose.

RM
Rahul Matthan9 of 11

We want to keep humans in the loop but if every AI output must be reviewed before it can be relied on, we will have moved the cost of intelligence from producing the answer to confirming it. We need to automate verification so that human work shifts to what must be judged.

RM
Rahul Matthan10 of 11

This is why absorption wins the race. A country that has crossed the frontier can make labs compete to serve it, holds the verified inference data only the far side produces, and, by owning the protocols that make its data legible, decides which model reads what and on what terms. Not a supplicant. A state that must be courted.

RM
Rahul Matthan11 of 11

None of this is immunity from the permanent periphery. It is resilience against it. The real gains will come when we tear up the workflows, org charts and institutions built around the old scarcity of human intelligence. The benefits of AI lie in what is yet to come.
